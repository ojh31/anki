What does the mnemonic <b>SAMPLE</b> stand for in Wilderness First Aid, and what is it used for?	<b>SAMPLE</b> is a patient history mnemonic used during a secondary assessment:<br><br><b>S</b> — Signs and Symptoms<br><b>A</b> — Allergies<br><b>M</b> — Medications<br><b>P</b> — Pertinent past medical history<br><b>L</b> — Last oral intake (food/water)<br><b>E</b> — Events leading up to the incident<br><br>It helps you gather the information needed to make an evacuation decision and guide ongoing care.
What does the mnemonic <b>AVPU</b> stand for, and when do you use it?	<b>AVPU</b> is a rapid scale for assessing mental status / level of responsiveness, used during the <b>Initial Assessment</b> (nervous-system corner) and for ongoing monitoring:<br><br><b>A</b> — Alert / Awake (oriented to person/place/time/event)<br><b>V</b> — responds to <b>Verbal</b> stimulus (talking, their name)<br><b>P</b> — responds to <b>Painful</b> stimulus only (e.g. pinch, sternal rub)<br><b>U</b> — Unresponsive / Unconscious (no response to verbal or painful stimulus)<br><br>A decline in AVPU is a red flag for a serious problem (e.g. head injury, shock, hypothermia) and warrants rapid evacuation.
What does the <b>STOPEATS</b> mnemonic stand for, and when do you use it?	Use STOPEATS when there's <b>no obvious trauma</b> — it prompts you to consider a <b>medical or environmental</b> cause:<br><br><b>S</b> — <b>Sugars</b> (hypo- / hyperglycemia)<br><b>T</b> — <b>Toxins</b> (poisons, food, animal vectors, alcohol, medications)<br><b>O</b> — <b>Oxygen</b> (hypoxia)<br><b>P</b> — <b>Pressure</b> (increased intracranial pressure)<br><b>E</b> — <b>Electricity</b> (seizure, electrical currents)<br><b>A</b> — <b>Altitude</b> (HAPE, HACE, nitrogen narcosis)<br><b>T</b> — <b>Temperature</b> (hypo- / hyperthermia)<br><b>S</b> — <b>Salts</b> (hyponatremia)
What does the <b>OPQRST</b> mnemonic stand for, and when do you use it?	<b>OPQRST</b> is a pain-assessment mnemonic used during the <b>Focused Exam &amp; History</b> for any pain complaint (chest, abdominal, headache, MSK, etc.):<br><br><b>O</b> — <b>Onset</b> — sudden vs. gradual; what was happening when it started<br><b>P</b> — <b>Provokes / Palliates</b> — what makes it worse or better (movement, food, position, pressure)<br><b>Q</b> — <b>Quality</b> — sharp, dull, cramping, burning, colicky, tearing<br><b>R</b> — <b>Region / Radiation</b> — where it is; whether it travels (e.g. flank → groin = kidney stone)<br><b>S</b> — <b>Severity</b> — 0–10 scale<br><b>T</b> — <b>Time</b> — duration; constant vs. intermittent; trend over time
What are the three phases of the NOLS <b>Patient Assessment System (PAS)</b>?	<b>1. Scene Size-up</b><br><b>2. Initial Assessment</b> — aka <b>"Stop &amp; Fix"</b> (life threats)<br><b>3. Focused Exam &amp; History</b>
In the NOLS PAS, what do you assess during the <b>Scene Size-up</b>?	• <b>Scene safety</b> (hazards, BSI / PPE)<br>• <b>MOI / NOI</b> — mechanism of injury or nature of illness<br>• <b>Number of patients</b> and resources needed<br><br>Done <i>before</i> approaching the patient.
What is the goal of the <b>Initial Assessment</b> (aka <b>Stop &amp; Fix</b>) in the NOLS PAS?	Find and treat immediate <b>life threats</b> — problems with <b>airway, breathing, or circulation</b> that will kill the patient within minutes if not corrected.<br><br>The name <i>Stop &amp; Fix</i> captures it: you pause everything else and fix life threats before moving on.
What does <b>ABCDE</b> stand for in the Initial Assessment?	<b>A</b> — Airway (with C-spine precautions as indicated)<br><b>B</b> — Breathing<br><b>C</b> — Circulation (pulse, perfusion, serious bleeding)<br><b>D</b> — Disability (level of responsiveness via AVPU, spine)<br><b>E</b> — Environment / Expose (protect from cold/heat; expose injuries as needed)
What three components make up the <b>Focused Exam &amp; History</b> (third phase of the NOLS PAS)?	<b>1. Physical exam</b> — head-to-toe, focused on findings<br><b>2. Vital signs</b> — baseline plus trends over time<br><b>3. SAMPLE history</b>
What does <b>MOI</b> stand for, and what does it describe?	<b>Mechanism of Injury</b> — the manner in which an <i>external physical force</i> caused an injury on the body.<br><br>Identified during the <b>Scene Size-up</b>; helps you detect likely <b>serious underlying injuries</b> (e.g. spinal, internal) before they're visible.
Which mechanisms of injury (MOIs) <b>transfer significant force</b> and should raise suspicion of serious underlying injury?	• Fall from <b>twice the patient's body height</b><br>• <b>Tumbling fall</b> — body rotates more than once<br>• <b>Compression or distraction</b> of the head &amp; neck<br>• <b>Blunt force</b> impact<br>• <b>Penetrations</b> by sharp objects<br>• <b>Rapid deceleration</b> into a stopped object (e.g. skier vs. tree)
What does <b>NOI</b> stand for, and what does it describe?	<b>Nature of Illness</b> — a medical condition you suspect may be affecting the patient due to <b>illness or environmental factors</b> (non-trauma).<br><br>Identified during the <b>Scene Size-up</b> to frame the initial impression for medical patients.
What general-impression clues suggest a <b>Nature of Illness (NOI)</b>?	• <b>Difficulty moving</b><br>• <b>Altered mental status</b><br>• <b>Difficulty breathing</b> — e.g. tripod posturing<br>• <b>Pain</b> with an unknown source<br>• <b>Abnormal appearance</b> — skin signs, grimace, odd speech<br>• Patient or peers say <i>"something's not right"</i>
What are the <b>three critical body systems</b> that must work together for survival, and what happens when one fails?	<b>1. Nervous</b> · <b>2. Respiratory</b> · <b>3. Circulatory</b><br><br>When any one of these systems fails, <b>the process of dying begins</b>.
What are the key <b>parts and function</b> of the <b>nervous system</b>?	<b>Parts:</b> brain &amp; spinal cord<br><b>Function:</b> automatically tells the <b>lungs to breathe</b> and the <b>heart to pump</b>.
What are the key <b>parts and function</b> of the <b>respiratory system</b>?	<b>Parts:</b><br>• Upper airway — nose &amp; mouth<br>• Lower airway — lungs &amp; alveoli<br><br><b>Function: oxygenation.</b> We breathe through the upper airway; oxygen is absorbed into the bloodstream via the lower airway (alveoli).
What are the key <b>parts and function</b> of the <b>circulatory system</b>?	<b>Parts (a closed-loop pump system):</b><br>• <b>Heart</b> — the pump<br>• <b>Blood vessels</b> — the pipes<br>• <b>Blood</b> — the fluid<br><br><b>Function: perfusion</b> — blood carries oxygenated blood throughout the body to vital organs (brain, lungs, other tissues). A problem with <i>any</i> of the three parts leads to <b>Shock</b>.
What are the three <b>underlying causes of death</b>, and which body system does each correspond to?	• <b>Circulation</b> → <b>Shock</b><br>• <b>Respiration</b> → <b>Respiratory Failure</b><br>• <b>Nervous</b> → <b>Brain Failure</b>
Define <b>Shock</b>	<b>Inadequate systemic perfusion</b> — the circulatory system fails to deliver oxygenated blood throughout the body.
Define <b>Respiratory Failure</b>	<b>Inadequate oxygenation</b> — the respiratory system fails to get enough oxygen into the bloodstream.
Define <b>Brain Failure</b>	<b>Inadequate oxygenation &amp; perfusion to a vital organ</b> (the brain).
What are the <b>three types of shock</b>, and which part of the circulatory system fails in each?	Each type corresponds to a failure in one part of the closed-loop pump system:<br><br>• <b>Fluid</b> (blood) fails → <b>Volume Shock</b><br>• <b>Pump</b> (heart) fails → <b>Cardiogenic Shock</b><br>• <b>Pipes</b> (blood vessels) fail → <b>Vascular Shock</b>
Define <b>Volume Shock</b>	<b>Fluid loss</b> from the circulatory system.
Mechanism of <b>Volume Shock</b>	External or internal <b>bleeding</b>, <b>vomiting</b>, <b>diarrhea</b>, <b>sweating</b>, <b>dehydration</b>, <b>burns</b>.
Define <b>Cardiogenic Shock</b>	<b>Failure of the heart to adequately pump oxygenated blood.</b>
Mechanism of <b>Cardiogenic Shock</b>	<b>Heart attack or heart failure</b> from clogged arteries, or <b>chest trauma</b>.
Define <b>Vascular Shock</b>	<b>Abnormal dilation of the blood vessels</b> — loss of the ability to regulate vessel tone.
Mechanism of <b>Vascular Shock</b>	<b>Spinal injury</b>, <b>anaphylaxis</b>, <b>poisoning</b>, <b>drug use</b>, <b>septic infection</b>.
Differential: <b>Compensated vs. Decompensated Shock</b>	<b>Compensated:</b> the body maintains perfusion by raising pulse and respiratory rate.<br><br><b>Decompensated</b> (aka "the crash"): the body can no longer compensate. <b>Life-threatening</b> — if the underlying cause isn't treated, the patient will die.
S/S of <b>Volume Shock</b> (vital sign pattern)	As fluid volume decreases:<br><br>• <b>Pulse</b> — rises, until the body can no longer compensate<br>• <b>Respiration</b> — rate rises; breathing becomes <b>shallow and labored</b><br>• <b>Skin</b> — becomes <b>pale</b> due to the <b>shell/core effect</b><br>• <b>Mental status</b> — normal at first, then a steady decline toward unconscious
Define the <b>shell/core effect</b>	As shock progresses, blood <b>shifts away from the skin (shell)</b> toward <b>vital organs (core)</b>. Makes the patient's skin look <b>pale, cool, clammy</b>.
Differential: <b>Volume vs. Vascular Shock</b> (vital signs)	Pulse and respiration still rise as the body tries to compensate — but because the vessels have lost the ability to regulate dilation/constriction, <b>there is no shell/core effect</b>. Skin may instead look <b>flushed and warm</b> as blood pools in peripheral vessels.
Differential: <b>Volume vs. Cardiogenic Shock</b> (vital signs)	The "pump" (heart) is the problem, so the pulse can be <b>rapid, slow, or irregular</b>. Otherwise vital signs are similar to Volume Shock.
Tx of <b>Shock</b> (general)	Treat the underlying cause as effectively as you can:<br><br>• <b>Stop fluid loss</b><br>• Give <b>sips of fluids</b> to rehydrate<br>• <b>Prevent heat loss</b> by insulating the patient<br>• Provide <b>supplemental oxygen</b><br>• <b>Monitor vital signs</b> and note changes<br>• <b>Evacuate quickly!</b><br><br><i>Early recognition is key to survival.</i>
Tx of <b>Volume Shock</b> (type-specific)	• <b>Direct pressure</b> to stop bleeding<br>• <b>Carefully rehydrate</b> the patient
Tx of <b>Cardiogenic Shock</b> (type-specific)	• Begin <b>CPR and assisted breathing</b> as needed<br>• Aid the patient in taking any <b>life-saving medications</b> — e.g. <b>Aspirin</b>, <b>Nitroglycerin</b>
Tx of <b>Vascular Shock</b> (type-specific)	• Treat the underlying <b>infection, poisoning, or illness</b><br>• Treat <b>anaphylaxis</b> with <b>Epinephrine</b> if protocols allow
Define <b>artery</b>	A blood vessel that carries <b>oxygenated blood away from the heart</b>.
Define <b>vein</b>	A blood vessel that <b>returns oxygen-poor blood back to the heart and lungs</b>.
Define <b>capillary</b>	A <b>small vessel</b> that delivers blood from arteries to the body's cells.
What are the main functions of the three <b>blood components</b>?	• <b>Red blood cells</b> — carry oxygen<br>• <b>White blood cells</b> — fight infection<br>• <b>Platelets</b> — clot blood
S/S of <b>arterial bleed</b>	<b>Appearance:</b> bright red, spurting<br><b>Severity:</b> <b>most serious</b> — difficult to control due to the high pressure from the heart's contractions.
S/S of <b>venous bleed</b>	<b>Appearance:</b> dark red, <b>oozing and flowing steadily</b>.
S/S of <b>capillary bleed</b>	<b>Appearance:</b> slow, leaking.<br><b>Severity:</b> not serious. Classic example: a nosebleed.
Tx of <b>critical bleed</b> (general sequence)	<b>1.</b> Don <b>PPE</b><br><b>2.</b> Quickly <b>expose and inspect</b> the wound; identify the source of bleeding<br><b>3.</b> Apply <b>well-aimed direct pressure</b> on the point of bleeding<br><b>4.</b> If bleeding is controlled → maintain it with a <b>pressure bandage</b><br><b>5.</b> If bleeding <i>cannot</i> be controlled with direct pressure → apply a <b>tourniquet</b>
What <b>PPE</b> should you wear when controlling a critical bleed?	• <b>Two pairs of gloves</b> — the extra layer gives you a clean pair if the outer is soaked or tears<br>• <b>Protective eyewear</b>
How do you apply <b>direct pressure</b> to a critical bleed, and what can you improvise with if you have no dressing?	Apply <b>well-aimed direct pressure</b> on the point of bleeding with your <b>fingers or the palm of your hand</b>.<br><br>If no pad or dressing is available, use <b>any clean fabric or cloth</b>, or a <b>gloved hand alone</b>.
Once direct pressure has controlled a bleed, how do you <b>maintain pressure</b>?	Apply a <b>pressure bandage</b>: place layers of gauze over the wound, then wrap an Ace bandage <b>tightly</b> over it to create continuous direct pressure.
When do you apply a <b>tourniquet</b>, and where do you place it?	<b>When:</b> bleeding <i>cannot</i> be controlled with direct pressure.<br><br><b>Placement:</b> <b>2 inches above the wound</b>, <b>proximal to the heart</b>.<br><br><b>Never</b> place a tourniquet over the <b>knee, elbow, or neck</b>.
Once a tourniquet is in place, what three things do you do?	<b>1.</b> <b>Monitor</b> the wound — ensure bleeding remains controlled<br><b>2.</b> Keep the tourniquet <b>visible</b> for future responders<br><b>3.</b> <b>Note the time</b> applied on the patient
How do you <b>improvise a tourniquet</b> if you have no commercial one?	Use a <b>bandage</b> plus an <b>unbreakable torsion device</b> — e.g. a tent pole, a sharpie, or several sticks taped together. Secure firmly and note the time applied.<br><br><b>Why "unbreakable":</b> if the torsion device breaks, the tourniquet releases and the built-up pressure makes <i>more</i> blood escape than before.
S/S of <b>internal bleeding</b>	Internal bleeding is <b>difficult to detect</b> — look for:<br><br>• A <b>significant or high-impact MOI</b><br>• <b>Tenderness</b> on the abdomen during the physical exam (palpate the <b>4 quadrants</b> and note findings)<br>• <b>Signs and symptoms of shock</b><br>• <b>Traumatic injury</b> to the <b>abdomen, torso, pelvis, or femur</b>
Define <b>guarding</b>	A protective response to abdominal pain: the patient <b>flexes their abdomen</b> and/or <b>hunches forward</b>. A clue to suspect <b>internal injury or bleeding</b>.
SAMPLE / pertinent questions for suspected <b>internal bleeding</b>	• Is the patient <b>guarding</b>?<br>• Is there any <b>bloody output</b>?<br>• Have they had <b>abdominal pain</b> for a prolonged time (<b>&gt;24 hrs</b>)?<br>• Is the patient <b>pregnant</b>?
Tx of <b>internal bleeding</b>	• <b>Treat for shock</b><br>• Keep the patient <b>warm and calm</b><br>• Provide <b>supplemental oxygen</b><br>• <b>Monitor vital sign</b> changes<br>• <b>Evacuate immediately!</b>
Define <b>respiration</b>	<b>Gas exchange</b> between the lungs and the bloodstream, taking place during <b>inspiration</b> (breathing in) and <b>expiration</b> (breathing out). Oxygen enters the blood via the <b>alveoli</b>; carbon dioxide leaves.
Differential: <b>Upper Airway Obstruction vs. Lower Airway Constriction</b> (by sound)	<b>Upper Airway Obstruction</b> — noisy breathing on <b>inspiration</b><br><b>Lower Airway Constriction</b> — noisy breathing on <b>expiration</b><br><br>The phase of the breath that sounds wrong localises the problem.
Mechanism of <b>Upper Airway Obstruction</b>	<b>Choking</b>; swollen <b>tongue, throat, or esophagus</b>.
S/S of <b>Upper Airway Obstruction</b>	<b>Wheezing</b>, <b>drooling</b>, noisy breathing on <b>inspiration</b>.
Mechanism of <b>Lower Airway Constriction</b>	Swelling of the <b>mucous membranes</b> and/or accumulation of <b>pus in the lungs</b> — <b>asthma</b>, <b>bronchitis</b>, <b>anaphylaxis</b>.
S/S of <b>Lower Airway Constriction</b>	<b>Tightness in the chest</b>; noisy breathing on <b>expiration</b>; <b>fever</b>.
What is the <b>progression</b> of untreated difficulty breathing?	<b>Respiratory Distress → Respiratory Failure → Respiratory Arrest</b><br><br>If not fixed, difficulty breathing will march through these stages to death.
S/S of <b>Respiratory Distress</b>	• Patient speaks in <b>short, choppy sentences</b><br>• Patient is <b>anxious</b><br>• <b>A</b> on the AVPU scale
S/S of <b>Respiratory Failure</b>	• Patient can only speak <b>1–2 words at a time</b><br>• Patient is <b>lethargic</b><br>• <b>A</b>, <b>V</b>, or <b>P</b> on the AVPU scale
S/S of <b>Respiratory Arrest</b>	• Patient is <b>no longer breathing</b><br>• <b>U</b> on the AVPU scale
Tx of <b>difficulty breathing</b>	• <b>Position of Comfort</b> — sit the patient in a position that allows them to breathe deeply<br>• <b>Reassurance</b> — keep the patient calm and comfortable<br>• <b>Oxygen</b> — remind the patient to take <b>long, deep, effective breaths</b><br>• <b>Positive Pressure Ventilation (PPV)</b> — assist breathing with a barrier device (e.g. pocket mask) if necessary<br><br>Use the <b>Head Tilt-Chin Lift</b> to open the airway if it is kinked or blocked.
What does <b>PPV</b> stand for, and when do you use it?	<b>Positive Pressure Ventilation</b> — assisting the patient's breathing by <b>pushing air into their lungs</b> using a <b>barrier device</b> (e.g. a <b>pocket mask</b>).<br><br>Used when the patient's own breathing is <b>inadequate</b>.
What happens <b>initially</b> when tissue or an organ is irritated or injured?	The tissue <b>leaks and/or swells</b>. The resulting <b>build-up or loss of fluid creates pressure</b>, and that pressure is what causes the problems (e.g. swelling and bruising at any injury site).
What is the <b>three-stage progression</b> of untreated tissue damage caused by swelling/fluid build-up?	<b>Ischemia → Infarction → Necrosis</b><br><br>Fluid build-up and swelling drive the progression.
Define <b>ischemia</b>	The <b>initial blockage</b> of blood flow to an area due to fluid or swelling.
Define <b>infarction</b>	<b>Inadequate local perfusion</b> — blood and oxygen can no longer reach the affected tissue.
Define <b>necrosis</b>	<b>Tissue death</b> due to continued infarction.
What is the time window before infarction becomes necrosis in a <b>limb</b>?	Approximately <b>2 hours</b>.
What is the time window before infarction becomes necrosis in the <b>brain</b>?	Only <b>5–6 minutes</b> — orders of magnitude shorter than a limb, which is why airway and circulation are the top priorities in the Initial Assessment.
Differential: <b>local vs. systemic infection</b> (example)	<b>Local infection:</b> pimple<br><b>Systemic infection:</b> sepsis
The <b>Initial Assessment</b> is a triangle with a corner for each critical body system. How many things do you assess at each corner?	<b>Two things per corner</b> — one pair for each of the three critical systems (circulatory, respiratory, nervous). Six items total.
In the Initial Assessment, what <b>two things</b> do you assess for the <b>circulatory system</b>, and what does each trigger?	• <b>Pulse</b> — if absent, begin <b>CPR</b><br>• <b>Critical bleeding</b> — control with <b>direct firm pressure</b>
In the Initial Assessment, what <b>two things</b> do you assess for the <b>respiratory system</b>, and what does each trigger?	• <b>Airway</b> — open using the <b>head-tilt, chin-lift</b><br>• <b>Breathing</b> — if inadequate, aid the patient with ventilations via a <b>pocket mask</b>
In the Initial Assessment, what <b>two things</b> do you assess for the <b>nervous system</b>?	• <b>AVPU</b> — mental status scale<br>• <b>Spinal precautions</b> — hold <b>C-spine</b> if significant MOI or any head/neck/spine pain
How does the <b>brain</b> send and receive messages throughout the body?	Via the <b>spinal cord</b>, which runs through the bony <b>vertebrae</b>. <b>Spinal cord roots</b> exit through openings in the vertebrae and travel as <b>nerves</b> that reach the rest of the body.
Why is a <b>spinal cord injury</b> so dangerous, and why are broken vertebrae especially concerning?	Even <b>small cuts to the spinal cord</b> can cause <b>permanent disability, paralysis, or death</b>.<br><br>A broken vertebra is a direct threat because <b>sharp bone edges</b> can cut into the spinal cord if the patient moves.
How many <b>vertebrae</b> are in each region of the spine?	• <b>Cervical</b> — 7<br>• <b>Thoracic</b> — 12<br>• <b>Lumbar</b> — 5<br>• <b>Sacral</b> — 5
What happens to the <b>sacral vertebrae</b> over time?	They begin to <b>fuse</b> anywhere from puberty into adulthood.
Mechanism of <b>spinal injury</b> (MOIs)	MOIs involving significant force to the head, neck, or spine:<br><br>• Falls from heights <b>twice body height</b><br>• <b>Compression / axial loading</b> — landing on the head or tailbone<br>• Excessive <b>flexion</b> — chin forced to chest<br>• Excessive <b>extension or rotation</b> — e.g. diving, tumbling falls<br>• <b>Distraction</b> of the head — e.g. attempted hanging<br>• <b>Penetration</b> — stabbing or impalement near the spine<br>• <b>Sudden deceleration</b> or impact to the body
S/S of <b>spinal injury</b>	• <b>Pain and tenderness</b> along the spine<br>• <b>Loss of CSM</b> in any of the 4 extremities<br>• <b>Numbness, tingling, or burning</b> sensations anywhere in the body
Tx of <b>spinal injury</b>	• <b>Immediately hold C-spine</b> and encourage the patient not to move<br>• If needed, <b>roll the patient onto their back</b><br>• <b>Call EMS</b> and await professional help if possible
If you must <b>move a patient with a suspected spinal injury</b> (for environmental, respiratory/circulatory, or safety reasons), how do you do it?	Move with the <b>spine in line</b> — keep the head, neck, and back aligned in a neutral position. Move the patient <b>as little as possible</b> while keeping the spine stable.
What does <b>TBI</b> stand for?	<b>Traumatic Brain Injury</b> — any <b>change in mental status</b> following head trauma (e.g. <b>loss of consciousness (LOC)</b> or lacking full orientation (<b>A&amp;Ox4</b>)).
What does <b>ICP</b> stand for?	<b>Increased Intracranial Pressure</b> — a more serious form of TBI; life-threatening.
Mechanism of <b>ICP</b>	Any irritated tissue swells or leaks fluid — with the brain, that swelling has <b>no room inside the rigid skull</b>, so pressure rises, compressing brain tissue.
S/S of <b>TBI</b> (early)	• <b>Altered mental status</b><br>• <b>Headache</b> and <b>nausea</b><br>• Any <b>deformity of the skull</b><br>• <b>Disoriented, irritable, confused, or combative</b> behavior
S/S of <b>TBI</b> (late-stage)	• <b>Loss of coordination</b> and/or <b>speech</b><br>• <b>Visual disturbances</b> — pupils <b>unequal</b><br>• <b>Fluid or blood</b> coming from <b>ears, eyes, or nose</b><br>• <b>Bruising</b> — <b>raccoon eyes</b> (around eyes) or <b>Battle's sign</b> (behind ears / around temples)
S/S of <b>ICP</b> (specific red flags during a progressing TBI)	• A <b>continually increasing headache</b><br>• <b>Persistent vomiting</b> — <b>3× or more</b>
SAMPLE / pertinent questions for suspected <b>TBI</b>	• Did the patient <b>lose consciousness</b>?<br>• Was the patient <b>wearing a helmet</b>?<br>• Has the patient had a <b>recent TBI</b>? When? How serious?
What does <b>SIS</b> stand for?	<b>Second Impact Syndrome</b> — when a person suffers <b>multiple TBIs in a short period of time</b>. Classic risk groups: boxers, football players, extreme athletes. <b>Can be fatal.</b>
Tx of <b>TBI / ICP</b>	• Suspect a possible <b>spinal injury</b> and <b>hold C-spine</b><br>• <b>Do NOT stop</b> the flow of fluid from the nose, mouth, or ears<br>• Consider the <b>airway</b> — be prepared to <b>suction</b> if vomit is present<br>• If the patient is vomiting, <b>secure them safely on their side</b><br>• <b>P.R.O.P.</b><br>• <b>Continually monitor and reassess</b><br>• <b>Evacuate quickly</b> if S/S of ICP develop
Define <b>stroke</b>	A <b>"brain attack"</b> caused by <b>clogged or ruptured blood vessels</b> in the brain.
What is the <b>STR</b> mnemonic for recognising a stroke?	<b>S — Smile:</b> have the patient smile. Is one side drooping?<br><b>T — Talk:</b> notice if the patient has slurred or difficult speech<br><b>R — Raise:</b> ask the patient to raise both arms. Are both sides moving equally?
Tx of <b>stroke</b>	• <b>Early recognition</b> using <b>STR</b><br>• <b>Do NOT give blood thinners!</b><br>• <b>Evacuate immediately</b>
When should you apply <b>C-spine precautions</b>?	Immediately, if the patient has:<br>• a <b>significant MOI</b>, <b>or</b><br>• <b>any pain</b> to the head, neck, or spine
What does it mean to <b>hold C-spine</b>?	Manually hold the patient's <b>head and neck in alignment with their back</b>, keeping the spine in a neutral line to protect the spinal cord from further injury.
How do you perform the <b>physical exam</b> in the Focused Exam &amp; History?	A <b>complete head-to-toe</b> examination <b>with physical contact</b> — don't just look, you have to <i>touch</i>.
During the head-to-toe physical exam, if you find a problem, where else should you check and why?	Look to the <b>skin</b> over the area for <b>bruising or bleeding</b> — signs of capillary leak or deeper underlying injury.
What four findings do you <b>look and feel for</b> during the head-to-toe physical exam?	<b>D</b>eformities<br><b>O</b>bstructions<br><b>T</b>enderness<br><b>S</b>welling<br><br>(Mnemonic: <b>DOTS</b>.)
What does <b>CSM</b> stand for (checked during the physical exam)?	<b>C</b>irculation · <b>S</b>ensation · <b>M</b>otion (aka Motor)
Where do you check <b>CSM</b> during the physical exam?	In each of the <b>four extremities</b> (both hands and both feet) — screens for neurovascular compromise distal to any potential injury.
What are the two <b>long bones</b> you're most likely to be asked about, and where are they?	• <b>Femur</b> — upper leg<br>• <b>Humerus</b> — upper arm
Define <b>ligament</b>	Connective tissue that connects <b>bone to bone</b>.
Define <b>tendon</b>	Connective tissue that connects <b>muscle to bone</b>.
Define <b>cartilage</b>	Connective tissue that is <b>softer than bone</b>; aids in <b>mobility</b> and <b>shock absorption</b>.
Define <b>fracture</b>	Any <b>discontinuity in bone tissue</b>.
Define <b>dislocation</b>	A <b>misalignment of bone at a joint</b>.
Define <b>sprain</b>	A <b>stretched or torn ligament</b>.
Define <b>strain</b>	A <b>stretched or torn tendon</b>.
How do you <b>assess a suspected musculoskeletal injury</b>?	• <b>Look</b> — remove clothing and observe the injury site; note any bruising, deformity, or abrasions<br>• <b>Ask</b> — MOI, pain level, range of motion, crepitus<br>• <b>Feel</b> — for deformity and swelling; compare to the <b>uninjured side</b><br>• <b>Check CSM</b> — circulation, sensation, motion
Evac for <b>musculoskeletal injury</b> (3 guiding questions)	• Is the injury <b>stable vs. unstable</b>?<br>• Is it severe and <b>affecting a joint or long bone necessary to walk</b>?<br>• Is there <b>CSM compromise</b>?
What does the <b>HIRICE</b> mnemonic stand for, and when is it used?	General Tx for stable and unstable musculoskeletal injuries:<br><br>• <b>H</b> — Hydrate<br>• <b>I</b> — Ibuprofen<br>• <b>R</b> — Rest<br>• <b>I</b> — Ice<br>• <b>C</b> — Compression<br>• <b>E</b> — Elevation
Tx of <b>stable MSK injury</b> (immobilization)	• After <b>20 min</b> of HIRICE, remove ice<br>• Wrap with an <b>ACE bandage</b> or other material — not so tight that it cuts off blood flow<br>• <b>Always wrap toward the heart</b><br>• Use a <b>sling/swathe</b> for upper-extremity injuries<br>• <b>Recheck CSM</b> after applying the bandage<br>• Give the patient something (stick, pole) for support while walking<br>• Evacuate carefully — don't worsen the injury
Tx of <b>unstable MSK injury</b> (immobilization)	<b>1.</b> Check <b>CSM</b><br><b>2.</b> <b>Manually stabilize</b> — have the patient hold their own stabilization if possible<br><b>3.</b> Prepare splinting materials<br><b>4.</b> <b>Measure splint</b> to the uninjured side<br><b>5.</b> <b>T.I.P.</b> (Traction Into Position)<br><b>6.</b> Apply the splint and secure it to the body — the splint must stabilize <b>above and below</b> the injury<br><b>7.</b> <b>Recheck CSM</b>
What does <b>T.I.P.</b> stand for, and when should you NOT use it?	<b>Traction Into Position</b> — applying gentle pull to bring a displaced long bone back toward anatomical alignment before splinting.<br><br><b>Do NOT T.I.P.</b> if a <b>joint is involved</b>.<br><br><b>Stop if:</b> you encounter <b>crepitus</b> or <b>gross resistance</b>, or if <b>pain increases</b>.
What are the key <b>principles of splinting</b>?	• No single splint works for every person or limb — <b>splinting is a creative process</b><br>• <b>Restrict motion without cutting off blood flow</b><br>• The splint must stabilize <b>above and below</b> the injury<br>• Always check <b>CSM before and after</b> applying the splint<br>• Build in a way to <b>re-assess</b> the injury and splint during evacuation, to avoid causing <b>ischemia → infarction → necrosis</b>
Tx of <b>open fracture</b>	<b>Evacuate!</b><br><br>• <b>Control any bleeding</b><br>• <b>Clean the exposed wound</b> carefully and thoroughly<br>• If you can't fully clean it, consider <b>leaving the injury exposed</b><br>• If evacuation is <b>&gt;4–6 hr</b>: <b>T.I.P. and splint</b><br><br><b>Tradeoff:</b> pulling bone ends back in <i>increases infection risk</i> but <i>increases survival of the bone tissue</i> (by restoring perfusion to the displaced segment).
Define the <b>epidermis</b> (and its function)	The <b>outermost layer</b> of skin.<br><br><b>Contains:</b> dead skin cells and bacteria<br><b>Function:</b> <b>protection from microbes</b>
Define the <b>dermis</b> (and its function)	The layer of skin <b>beneath the epidermis</b>.<br><br><b>Contains:</b> blood vessels, glands, hair, nerves<br><b>Function:</b> <b>thermoregulation</b>
What is the role of the <b>fat layer</b> of the skin?	<b>Energy storage</b> and <b>insulation</b>.
Define <b>fascia</b> (and its function)	A layer of <b>connective tissue</b> that <b>covers muscle, bones, organs, and joints</b>.
What four <b>functions of the skin</b> are compromised by a wound?	• <b>Protection</b><br>• <b>Sensation</b><br>• <b>Thermoregulation</b><br>• <b>Fluid retention</b>
What are the <b>six types of wounds</b>?	• <b>Lacerations</b><br>• <b>Abrasions</b><br>• <b>Avulsions</b><br>• <b>Amputations</b><br>• <b>Evisceration</b><br>• <b>Burns</b>
Differential: <b>High-risk vs. Low-risk wound</b>	<b>High-risk:</b><br>• Penetrates the <b>fascia</b><br>• <b>Heavily contaminated</b><br>• <b>Open fractures</b><br>• <b>Bites, punctures, or impalement</b><br>• Burns to the <b>face, genitals, or large areas</b><br>• Involves the <b>face, eyes, or mouth</b><br>• Needs <b>aggressive field management</b><br>• <b>Difficult evacuation</b> or travel<br><br><b>Low-risk:</b><br>• Involves only the <b>dermis</b><br>• <b>Small / clean avulsions</b><br>• <b>Small lacerations</b><br>• <b>Blisters / chafing</b><br>• <b>Small-area burns</b><br>• No functional, cosmetic, or life risk<br>• <b>Manageable in the field</b><br>• <b>Easy evacuation/travel</b>
Tx of <b>low-risk wound</b>	<b>1.</b> Wear appropriate <b>PPE</b><br><b>2. Clean</b> — high-pressure irrigation with <b>clean water or iodine solution</b><br><b>3. Cover</b> — don't seal in bacteria; extend gauze about <b>1 inch past the wound</b> on all sides<br><br><b>Prevent infection:</b> clean thoroughly. <b>Do it right the first time!</b>
Tx of <b>high-risk wound</b>	• <b>Direct pressure</b> to stop the bleed<br>• <b>High-pressure irrigation</b> to remove debris<br>• <b>Do NOT close the wound</b> — closing can trap debris and cause infection<br>• Apply <b>moist gauze</b> saturated with <b>saline and/or antibiotic ointment</b><br>• Cover the moist dressing with <b>dry dressings</b> and bandage in place<br>• <b>Splint</b> the extremity to reduce movement<br>• <b>Anticipate shock</b> from blood loss and/or infection
Define <b>avulsion</b>	The <b>incomplete severing</b> of a body part — a flap of tissue remains attached.
Tx of <b>avulsion</b>	• Control bleeding with <b>direct pressure</b><br>• <b>P.R.O.P.</b> and treat for shock if necessary<br>• Once bleeding is controlled, <b>clean and bandage</b> the wound with the skin flap <b>in place</b>
Define <b>amputation</b>	The <b>complete severing</b> of a body part.
Tx of <b>amputation</b>	• Control bleeding with <b>direct pressure</b><br>• Once bleeding is controlled, <b>clean and bandage</b> the wound<br>• <b>P.R.O.P.</b> and treat for shock if necessary<br>• Locate and preserve the severed part
How do you care for a <b>severed body part</b> after an amputation?	• Wrap it in <b>saline-soaked sterile cloth</b><br>• Place it in a <b>sealed waterproof container</b><br>• <b>Keep cool:</b> place the sealed bag inside <i>another</i> bag of <b>ice water</b><br>• <b>Never</b> place the severed part <b>directly on ice or snow</b> (freezing injury kills the tissue)
Define <b>evisceration</b>	An abdominal injury in which <b>organs protrude out</b> of the wound. The exposed organs may still be <b>functioning</b> — handle very carefully.
Tx of <b>evisceration</b>	• <b>Do NOT</b> push organs back into the body<br>• <b>Do NOT</b> apply direct pressure<br>• Cover organs with a <b>thick, moist dressing</b> or saline-soaked abdominal pad<br>• <b>Evacuate immediately!</b>
Define <b>insulin</b> (and its function)	A <b>hormone</b> secreted by the <b>pancreas</b> that helps <b>glucose transfer into cells</b>, where it is stored and converted into energy.
Define <b>diabetic emergency</b>	An <b>imbalance between glucose and insulin</b> — a diabetic is unable to produce an adequate amount of insulin, so the body's cells don't receive enough energy from sugar.
Differential: <b>Hypoglycemia vs. Hyperglycemia</b>	<b>Hypoglycemia</b> — low blood sugar; progresses <b>rapidly</b> and can become <b>life-threatening</b>.<br><br><b>Hyperglycemia</b> — high blood sugar; progresses <b>slowly</b> over a long period.
In a wilderness setting, why do we <b>"assume hypoglycemia"</b>, and what's the key rule?	We burn more fuel in the wilderness, using up sugar faster — so we're much closer to <b>low</b> blood sugar than high.<br><br><b>Hypoglycemia kills faster</b> than hyperglycemia — withholding sugar from a hypoglycemic patient can mean <b>permanent brain damage or death</b>.<br><br><b>Always give sugar when in doubt.</b>
Mechanism of <b>hypoglycemia</b> (causes)	• <b>Too much insulin</b><br>• <b>Not eating enough</b> food or sugars<br>• <b>Overexertion</b><br>• <b>Pancreas failure</b>
S/S of <b>hypoglycemia</b>	• <b>Slurred speech</b>, inability to communicate<br>• <b>Altered mental status</b><br>• <b>Trembling, shaking</b>, loss of coordination<br>• <b>Weakness, fainting</b><br>• <b>Pale, cool, sweaty</b> skin<br>• Progression: <b>seizures</b> → <b>U</b> on AVPU
History clues for a suspected <b>diabetic emergency</b>	• <b>Medical alert bracelet</b><br>• <b>Insulin kit</b><br>• Ask <b>friends or family</b>
Tx of <b>hypoglycemia</b> (conscious patient who can swallow)	• Provide <b>oral glucose</b><br>• If not available, give <b>easy-to-absorb sugars</b> — orange juice, candy, fruit<br>• <b>Artificial sweeteners will NOT help</b>
Tx of <b>hypoglycemia</b> (unresponsive or unable to swallow)	• Place the patient in the <b>recovery position</b> (on their side)<br>• <b>Protect and maintain the airway</b><br>• <b>Repeatedly place small amounts of oral glucose or sugar</b> under the tongue or between gums and teeth<br>• Be very careful to <b>prevent aspiration</b><br>• <b>Evacuate immediately!</b> Life-threatening condition.
What are the <b>four routes</b> by which toxins can enter the body?	Toxins can be:<br>• <b>Ingested</b><br>• <b>Inhaled</b><br>• <b>Absorbed</b><br>• <b>Injected</b>
For <b>any toxin exposure</b>, what four general areas should you consider?	• <b>Patient factors</b> — age (older &amp; younger are more susceptible); SAMPLE for co-existing conditions and medications<br>• <b>Evacuation</b> — distance to care; does the hospital have <b>antivenin</b>? Terrain / energy cost<br>• <b>Toxic overload</b> — how much of the toxin? how long was the patient exposed?<br>• <b>Identification</b> — identify or <b>photograph the vector/substance</b>
What is the <b>National Poison Control Hotline</b> number, and what should you tell them?	<b>1-800-222-1222</b><br><br>Clearly <b>identify the substance</b> and provide details about the incident.
Common sources of <b>ingested toxins</b>	• Overdosed <b>medications</b><br>• <b>Personal care and cleaning products</b><br>• <b>Fuels</b><br>• <b>Plants and mushrooms</b><br>• <b>Spoiled food</b>
Tx of <b>ingested toxins</b>	• Identify the substance and <b>contact Poison Control</b><br>• Consider <b>activated charcoal</b> if available<br>• <b>Do NOT give water</b> unless advised by Poison Control<br>• <b>P.R.O.P.</b><br>• <b>Evacuate</b>
S/S of <b>plant or mushroom poisoning</b>	<b>S/S:</b><br>• Nausea and vomiting<br>• Diarrhea<br>• Abdominal pain<br>• Headache<br>• Hallucinations<br><br><b>Timing:</b> symptoms may appear <b>up to 12 hours after ingestion</b>. Can progress to <b>kidney/liver failure</b> and death.
Common sources of <b>inhaled toxins</b> in a wilderness setting	• <b>Smoke</b> from burning poison oak or other caustic substances<br>• <b>Carbon monoxide</b> — e.g. from cooking in an unventilated snow cave or shelter<br>• <b>Chlorine gas</b> — e.g. from mixing bleach and vinegar
Tx of <b>inhaled toxins</b>	• Get the patient to <b>fresh air immediately</b><br>• <b>Anticipate respiratory distress</b><br>• <b>P.R.O.P.</b><br>• <b>Contact Poison Control</b><br>• <b>Evacuate</b> if necessary
Tx of <b>absorbed toxins</b>	• <b>Immediately clean</b> the affected area<br>• <b>Soaps or vinegar</b> may be needed to remove oily substances<br>• <b>Leave blisters intact</b><br>• <b>Contact Poison Control</b><br><br><b>High risk</b> if <b>&gt;10% of body surface area</b> is affected:<br>• <b>P.R.O.P.</b><br>• <b>Evacuate</b>
Mechanism of <b>poison oak</b>	<b>Urushiol oil</b> — present in <i>all</i> parts of the plant, not just the leaves.<br>Mantra: <b>"Leaves of three, let it be."</b>
S/S of <b>poison oak</b>	Irritated, red, itchy, inflamed skin. Can spread to others or to new body parts via scratching/touching.
Tx of <b>poison oak</b>	• <b>Irrigate immediately</b><br>• Wash with <b>soapy water</b>, directing runoff <i>away from</i> non-affected body parts<br>• Treat with <b>calamine lotion</b> or a specialized wash (e.g. <b>Tecnu</b>)
Mechanism of <b>stinging nettle</b>	<b>Histamines</b> plus tiny <b>spikes</b> that irritate the skin.
S/S of <b>stinging nettle</b>	Itching, burning, rash, small blisters. Lasts <b>hours to days</b>.
Tx of <b>stinging nettle</b>	• <b>Wash</b> the exposed area immediately<br>• Apply a <b>cool compress</b> to alleviate pain
Common sources of <b>injected toxins</b>	• <b>Needle sticks</b><br>• <b>Insects and arachnids</b> (vectors)<br>• <b>Marine animals</b>
Tx of <b>injected toxins</b> (general)	<b>Prevention first:</b><br>• <b>Know your environment</b> — research vectors before traveling<br>• Prepare with proper clothing, medications, and a plan<br><br><b>If exposed:</b><br>• <b>Identify</b> the vector — take a picture<br>• <b>Contact Poison Control</b><br>• <b>Monitor vital signs</b>; watch for developing <b>fever</b><br>• <b>P.R.O.P.</b><br>• <b>Evacuate</b> if necessary
S/S of <b>snake bite</b>	• <b>Tissue swelling</b> at the injection site<br>• <b>Paralysis, cramping, numbness</b> if neurotoxin
Tx of <b>snake bite</b>	• <b>Contact Poison Control</b><br>• <b>Splint</b> the extremity if possible<br>• <b>P.R.O.P.</b><br>• <b>Evacuate</b> to a hospital with <b>antivenin</b>
What must you <b>NOT</b> do for a snake bite?	<b>DO NOT:</b> attempt <b>suction</b>, apply a <b>tourniquet</b>, or use <b>ice</b>.
Tx of <b>tick exposure</b>	<b>Check every night</b> for ticks — especially in <b>dark, moist areas</b> of the body and <b>in hair</b>.<br><br>If found, remove <b>completely</b> with <b>tweezers</b>.
Evac for <b>tick exposure</b>	If a <b>red mark, bullseye rash, or fever</b> develops → <b>Evacuate</b>.
Define <b>allergic reaction</b> (and routes of exposure)	The body's response to an <b>allergen</b> or foreign substance — the immune system releases <b>histamine</b> and other substances to confront and remove it.<br><br><b>Routes of exposure</b> (same as toxins): <b>ingested, inhaled, injected, absorbed</b>.
Common <b>allergens</b>	• <b>Foods and medications</b><br>• <b>Animal dander</b><br>• <b>Insect venom</b><br>• <b>Plants and pollen</b><br>• <b>Latex</b> and other materials
S/S of <b>minor to moderate allergic reaction</b>	• <b>Local reaction</b> — swelling and tenderness at the exposure site<br>• <b>Flushed, itchy skin</b><br>• <b>Watery eyes</b>; irritated, <b>stuffy nose</b>
Tx of <b>minor to moderate allergic reaction</b>	• <b>Oral antihistamine</b><br>&nbsp;&nbsp;— Generic: <b>Diphenhydramine</b><br>&nbsp;&nbsp;— Brand: <b>Benadryl</b>, <b>Claritin</b><br>• <b>Ice or cool</b> the affected area<br>• <b>Anti-itch ointment</b>
Define <b>anaphylaxis</b>	A <b>severe, life-threatening allergic reaction</b> — the body responds to an allergen with an excessive response.
S/S of <b>anaphylaxis</b>	• <b>Difficulty breathing</b> — constriction of the airway<br>• <b>Swelling</b> of the face, eyelids, lips, tongue, and soft tissue of the throat<br>• <b>Systemic hives</b>, particularly on the face and chest<br>• <b>Nausea</b> and abdominal cramping<br>• A <b>drop in blood pressure</b> due to dilation of the blood vessels
How do you administer an <b>epinephrine auto-injector</b> in an anaphylaxis emergency?	• Dose: <b>0.3 mg</b><br>• Inject into the <b>middle, lateral side</b> of the patient's thigh<br>• Remove the injector and <b>gently rub</b> the injection site
Tx of <b>anaphylaxis</b> (Wilderness Protocol)	• <b>Epinephrine auto-injector</b> — 0.3 mg, mid-lateral thigh<br>• If the patient can swallow, give an <b>oral antihistamine</b> (Diphenhydramine)<br>• <b>P.R.O.P.</b><br>• <b>Second dose</b> of epinephrine after <b>10 minutes</b> if patient doesn't respond<br>• <b>Evacuate immediately</b> — even if the patient appears to recover
Evac for <b>anaphylaxis</b> (why even after apparent recovery?)	The patient may <b>relapse</b>, and the condition can become <b>fatal if more doses of epinephrine are unavailable</b>. Evacuation ensures access to further doses and definitive medical care.
Define <b>hypoxia</b>	A <b>deficiency in the amount of oxygen reaching the tissues</b>.<br><br>Can be a symptom of many different problems causing difficulty breathing.
Define <b>asthma</b>	A condition in which certain <b>substances, exertion, or environmental factors</b> trigger <b>inflammation of the airways</b>, causing difficulty breathing.
Define <b>inhaler</b>	A <b>portable device</b> used by asthmatics to deliver a <b>measured amount of inhaled medicine</b> from a pressurized canister.
S/S of <b>asthma attack</b>	• <b>Wheezing</b> and <b>coughing</b><br>• A feeling of <b>tightness in the chest</b><br>• <b>Shortness of breath (S.O.B.)</b>
Tx of <b>asthma attack</b>	• Have the patient <b>relax</b> and <b>stop vigorous activity</b><br>• <b>Assist the patient with their inhaler</b>
S/S of <b>severe asthma</b> (Wilderness Protocol)	• <b>Persistent altered mental status</b><br>• <b>Incomplete response</b> to treatment via inhaler<br>• Patient's condition is <b>worsening</b>, progressing toward <b>respiratory arrest</b>
Tx of <b>severe asthma</b> (not controllable with an inhaler)	• <b>P.R.O.P.</b><br>• <b>Epinephrine auto-injector 0.3 mg</b> — via lateral thigh<br>• <b>Rescue inhaler</b> if available<br>• <b>Immediate evacuation</b>
Mechanism of <b>lightning injury</b> (6 routes)	• <b>Direct strike</b> — lightning directly hits the person<br>• <b>Splash current</b> — lightning jumps from its target to a person<br>• <b>Ground current</b> — the charge radiates out from the strike point to a person<br>• <b>Long conductors</b> — someone touches a fence or other conductor that is hit<br>• <b>Blast injury</b> — person thrown by the exploding air caused by lightning<br>• <b>Penetrating injury</b> — person injured by debris
S/S of <b>lightning injury</b>	• <b>Altered mental status</b> — TBI, LOC, paralysis, seizures<br>• <b>Blindness</b> or <b>deafness</b> — permanent or temporary<br>• <b>Burns</b> — superficial or internal<br>• <b>Blunt and penetrating trauma</b>
Tx of <b>lightning injury</b> (responsive patient)	• <b>Treat any visible burns</b><br>• <b>Anticipate airway, cardiac, and neurological complications</b><br>• <b>Evacuate</b>
Tx of <b>lightning injury</b> (unresponsive patient)	• <b>Immediately begin CPR</b> with compressions<br>• <b>Early AED defibrillation may save a life</b> in this situation
Scene-safety priorities at a <b>lightning strike</b>	• <b>Continually ensure the scene is safe</b> — lightning may strike twice in the same place<br>• <b>Leave water</b> and <b>put on shoes</b> if on wet ground<br>• Move to a <b>lower, covered</b> area<br>• <b>Smaller strands of trees</b> are safer than large exposed ones<br>• <b>Caves</b> may conduct electricity — <i>not</i> a recommended shelter
How should you <b>position yourself</b> if caught in a lightning storm, and what should a group do?	<b>Individual:</b><br>• Create a <b>barrier between you and the ground</b> — something rubber or non-conductive (e.g. a sleeping mat)<br>• <b>Crouch down</b> and <b>cover your ears</b><br><br><b>Group plan:</b><br>• <b>Separate</b> and <b>number off</b> — so you can account for everyone after the storm
Mechanism of <b>altitude illness</b> (and acclimatization)	<b>Cause:</b> declining <b>oxygen content</b> in the air and decreasing <b>barometric pressure</b> cause <b>hypoxia</b>.<br><br><b>Acclimatization:</b> the body changes the <b>pH of the blood</b>, and <b>HR ↑</b> and <b>RR ↑</b>.
At what altitude can <b>AMS</b> occur, and what factors affect susceptibility?	AMS can occur as low as <b>8,000 ft</b>.<br><br>Susceptibility varies with:<br>• <b>Age</b> — young and old are more affected<br>• <b>Physical fitness</b><br>• <b>Medical history</b><br>• <b>Genetic predisposition</b><br>• Consumption of <b>alcohol or drugs</b>
S/S of <b>AMS</b>	• <b>Headache</b><br>• <b>Loss of appetite</b><br>• <b>Nausea, vomiting</b><br>• <b>Insomnia</b><br>• <b>Weariness, exhaustion</b>
Tx of <b>AMS</b>	• <b>Descend</b> and <b>acclimatize</b> until symptoms resolve<br>• <b>Supplemental oxygen</b><br><br>If not addressed, AMS can progress to the life-threatening conditions <b>HAPE</b> and <b>HACE</b>.
What does <b>HAPE</b> stand for?	<b>High Altitude Pulmonary Edema</b>.<br><br>Can be <b>misdiagnosed as bronchitis or pneumonia</b>.
Mechanism of <b>HAPE</b>	The heart is unable to provide adequate blood flow at altitude, so fluid <b>backs up and leaks from the capillaries of the alveoli</b> into the air spaces of the lungs.
S/S of <b>HAPE</b>	• <b>Shortness of breath</b>, <b>dry cough</b><br>• <b>Chest pain</b><br>• Cough may become productive — <b>frothy, reddish sputum</b><br>• <b>Low-grade fever</b>
Tx of <b>HAPE</b>	• <b>Descend ASAP</b><br>• <b>Supplemental oxygen</b><br>• <b>Gamow bag</b> or <b>hyperbaric chamber</b> for serious evacuation cases<br>• <b>Diamox</b> — medication that can be prescribed before high-elevation adventures
What does <b>HACE</b> stand for?	<b>High Altitude Cerebral Edema</b>.
Mechanism of <b>HACE</b>	At altitude, fluid <b>leaks from cerebral capillaries</b> and collects within the brain. The rigid skull gives the swelling no room → <b>increased intracranial pressure (ICP)</b>.
S/S of <b>HACE</b>	• <b>Headache</b> not relieved by rest or medication<br>• <b>Loss of coordination</b><br>• <b>Altered mental status</b>, <b>seizures</b>, <b>coma</b><br>• <b>Nausea</b>
Tx of <b>HACE</b>	• <b>Descend ASAP</b><br>• <b>Supplemental oxygen</b><br>• <b>Hydration</b><br>• <b>Gamow bag</b> or <b>hyperbaric chamber</b><br>• <b>Diamox</b>
Evac for <b>HAPE / HACE</b>	<b>Immediately descend at least 1,000–1,500 ft</b> to relieve symptoms.<br><br>Follow with <b>complete evacuation to professional medical care</b>. <b>Go fast!</b>
Prevention of <b>altitude illness</b>	<b>Prevention is the best treatment:</b><br>• Plan a <b>staged ascent</b><br>• <b>Allow the body to adjust</b> before increasing physical demand<br>• <b>"Climb high, sleep low"</b><br>• Eat a <b>carbohydrate-rich diet</b> and <b>stay hydrated</b><br>• Consider carrying <b>Diamox</b> or other prescribed altitude meds
When assessing <b>skin signs</b>, what three things do you evaluate and what descriptors are used for each?	<b>CTM</b> — assessed as part of vitals:<br><br>• <b>C</b>olor — pink, pale, flushed, cyanotic (blue), jaundiced (yellow), mottled, ashen<br>• <b>T</b>emperature — hot, warm, cool, cold (use the back of your hand on the forehead or chest)<br>• <b>M</b>oisture — dry, moist, clammy, diaphoretic
Differential: <b>Normal vs. Shock skin signs</b>	<b>Normal:</b> pink, warm, dry (<b>PWD</b>)<br><b>Shock:</b> pale, cool, clammy<br><br>A shift away from PWD is an early clue that perfusion or oxygenation is failing.
What are the NOLS <b>4 P's of Safety</b>, in priority order?	A priority-ordered safety check during <b>Scene Size-up</b>:<br><br><b>1. Personal</b> — your own safety first<br><b>2. Partner</b> — your rescue team<br><b>3. Public</b> — bystanders and other people on scene<br><b>4. Patient</b> — the patient last<br><br>Rationale: you can't help anyone if you become a second patient, so rescuers come before the patient.
Why are the NOLS 4 P's of Safety ordered <b>Personal → Partner → Public → Patient</b> rather than patient-first?	Because a rescuer who becomes a second patient doubles the problem and removes a helper. Prioritising your own safety, then your team's, then bystanders', preserves the resources needed to actually help the patient.
What does <b>PPE</b> stand for?	<b>Personal Protective Equipment.</b><br><br>Personal safety (the first of the 4 P's) begins with donning PPE before patient contact.
What does <b>BSI</b> stand for?	<b>Body Substance Isolation</b> — the principle of creating barriers between you and a patient's bodily fluids (blood, vomit, saliva, etc.) to prevent pathogen transmission.
What four items make up <b>PPE</b>, and when is each used?	• <b>Gloves</b> — always, for any patient contact<br>• <b>Face shield or particulate mask</b> — if respiratory-illness threats are present<br>• <b>Protective eyewear</b> — if spurting blood, vomit, or coughing is likely<br>• <b>Protective gown</b> — for large-area BSI from any bodily fluids
What can you use for hand protection if you don't have nitrile gloves?	Improvise a barrier: ski gloves, leather gloves, plastic bags, or any piece of plastic. Any barrier is better than bare hands.
What key principle about contagion should you assume with every patient?	<b>Assume everyone is contagious.</b><br><br>Anyone — regardless of age, race, gender, or demographic — can carry airborne or blood-borne pathogens <i>without showing any signs or symptoms</i>.
Which three <b>blood-borne pathogens</b> are of greatest concern in WFR?	<b>Hepatitis B</b>, <b>Hepatitis C</b>, and <b>HIV</b>.
When sizing up the scene, what four questions should you ask to <b>assess the numbers</b>?	• How many <b>patients vs. trained responders</b>?<br>• How many <b>helpful hands</b> are available for an evacuation?<br>• What <b>resources</b> do you have to aid evacuation?<br>• How far are you from the <b>trailhead or additional resources</b>?<br><br>Shapes your plan, pace, and evacuation decisions.
Define <b>triage</b>	The process of categorising <b>patient priority</b> when you have <b>many patients</b> (more casualties than responders can treat at once).
How do you determine <b>patient priority</b> during triage?	By assessing <b>high-risk vs. low-risk</b> injury or illness. High-risk patients are treated and evacuated first.
Define <b>thermoregulation</b>	The body's ability to <b>regulate heat</b> internally and in response to <b>environmental factors</b>.
What is the <b>baseline body temperature</b>?	<b>37°C</b> / <b>98.6°F</b>
What are the <b>four components</b> of thermoregulation, and what does each do?	• <b>Muscles</b> — generate heat by <b>shivering</b><br>• <b>Skin</b> — vessels <b>constrict</b> to preserve heat or <b>dilate</b> to dissipate heat; <b>sweat glands</b> dissipate heat<br>• <b>Endocrine system</b> — controls <b>metabolism</b> to regulate heat production<br>• <b>Temperature sensors</b> — send info to the <b>brain</b> to adjust regulation
What are the <b>four mechanisms of environmental heat transfer / loss</b>?	• <b>Evaporation</b> — sweating, breath<br>• <b>Radiation</b> — heat waves, body heat<br>• <b>Convection</b> — air currents, wind, water<br>• <b>Conduction</b> — cold surfaces pull heat
What two problems occur when thermoregulation <b>fails</b> and environmental factors aren't addressed?	• <b>Hypothermia</b> — low body temperature<br>• <b>Hyperthermia</b> — high body temperature
When does a normal body <b>fail to compensate</b> for thermal stress? (5 factors)	• <b>Alcohol</b><br>• <b>Age</b> — very old or very young<br>• <b>Medication</b><br>• <b>Illness or injury</b><br>• <b>Environmental extremes</b>
Define <b>hypothermia</b>	Body temperature lowers until <b>normal brain &amp; muscle function are impaired</b>. The body strives to balance heat gain/loss to keep core temperature around <b>98.6°F</b>.
Who is at <b>higher risk</b> for hypothermia?	• <b>Children</b> — larger <b>BSA-to-mass ratio</b><br>• <b>Elderly</b> — decreased thermoregulation<br>• <b>Chronic / pre-existing</b> conditions that inhibit cold response<br>• <b>Trauma, drugs, illness</b> — compromise thermoregulation
Define <b>acute hypothermia</b>	<b>Rapid onset</b> (e.g. cold water submersion).<br><br>• Patient is usually <b>not calorie depleted or dehydrated</b><br>• <b>Spontaneous rewarming</b> is possible
Define <b>subacute hypothermia</b>	<b>Slow onset</b> (e.g. backcountry skiing).<br><br>• Patient is <b>calorie depleted &amp; dehydrated</b><br>• <b>Careful rewarming</b> required; may need <b>hospital treatment</b>
What is the <b>prevention principle</b> for hypothermia, and when do you act?	<b>Preventing a decrease in core temperature is easier than raising it.</b><br><br>When <i>anyone</i> in the group is <b>cold, wet, or shivering</b>, <b>address it as a group immediately</b>.
S/S of <b>Moderate Hypothermia</b>	• <b>Uncontrollable shivering</b><br>• <b>Altered mental status</b><br>• <b>Slurred speech</b><br>• Altered motor skills — <b>the umbles</b>
What are <b>the umbles</b>, and what do they indicate?	<b>Fumbling, grumbling, mumbling, stumbling</b> — the cluster of <b>altered motor and mental skills</b> seen in moderate hypothermia.
Tx of <b>Moderate Hypothermia</b>	• <b>Move slowly and gently</b> to a warmer place (mind convection, evaporation, conduction, radiation)<br>• <b>Remove wet clothes</b>, replace with warm/dry layers<br>• <b>Actively rewarm</b> — apply heat to the <b>upper torso</b> (chest, armpits, back)<br>• Create a <b>hypothermia wrap</b><br>• Provide hydration and easy-to-consume foods — <b>warm sugar water</b><br>• Consider <b>evacuation</b> if field rewarming will be difficult or insufficient
S/S of <b>Severe Hypothermia</b>	• <b>Shivering stops</b>; muscles become <b>rigid</b><br>• Altered mental status declines — <b>stupor progressing to unconscious</b><br>• <b>↓ HR, ↓ RR</b><br>• <b>Pale, blue skin</b>
Tx of <b>Severe Hypothermia</b>	<b>Life-threatening — gentle but urgent evacuation.</b><br><br>• Controlled rewarming in a <b>hospital</b> may be necessary<br>• Create a <b>hypothermia wrap</b>; add <b>gentle heat sources</b> to chest <i>inside</i> the wrap<br>• <b>PPV with warm air</b><br>• <b>Do NOT give foods or fluids</b>
Define <b>heat cramps</b>	<b>Sudden muscle spasms</b> caused by the body's <b>deficiency in sodium or other electrolytes</b>. The <b>first stage</b> of heat-related illness.
Tx of <b>early heat illness</b> (prevention / heat cramps)	<b>Prevention and early recognition are the best treatments.</b><br><br>• <b>Stop activity</b><br>• <b>Replenish fluids</b><br>• <b>Consume salts and electrolytes</b><br><br>If not addressed, heat illness will progress to a <b>life-threatening condition</b>.
S/S of <b>Heat Exhaustion</b>	• <b>Pale, sweaty, flushed</b> skin<br>• <b>Decreased urine output</b>; <b>dark yellow urine</b><br>• <b>Headache, dizziness, weakness</b><br>• <b>Dehydration, thirst</b><br>• <b>Nausea, vomiting</b><br>• <b>↑ HR &amp; ↑ RR</b><br>• <b>↑ or normal</b> core temperature
Tx of <b>Heat Exhaustion</b>	• <b>Stop activity</b><br>• Use the <b>4 heat-transfer mechanisms</b> (evaporation, radiation, convection, conduction):<br>&nbsp;&nbsp;– Loosen or remove excess clothing<br>&nbsp;&nbsp;– Lie/sit patient on the ground to rest<br>• Apply <b>aggressive cooling</b><br>• Encourage continued <b>small sips of fluids and electrolytes</b><br>• Patient may <b>resume activity when ready</b>
Define <b>Heat Stroke</b> (and what type of shock is it?)	Thermoregulation systems <b>fail</b>; the body overheats and <b>permanent brain damage</b> can occur, potentially leading to death.<br><br>Heat Stroke is a form of <b>Volume Shock</b>.
S/S of <b>Heat Stroke</b>	• <b>Red, flushed, dry</b> skin — <b>warm or hot</b> to the touch<br>• <b>Altered mental status</b> — confusion, odd behavior, difficulty communicating<br>• <b>Severe headache, seizure, unconsciousness</b><br>• <b>↑ HR; ↑ RR &amp; shallow</b><br>• <b>↑ core body temperature</b><br>• <b>↓ urine output</b>; <b>dark urine</b>
Tx of <b>Heat Stroke</b>	<b>Aggressive cooling is required.</b><br><br>• If patient is <b>alert &amp; oriented</b> → <b>lower-body submersion in cold water</b><br>• Place <b>cold packs</b> on <b>neck, groin, and armpits</b><br>• <b>Anticipate relapse</b><br>• <b>Evacuate</b>
Define <b>hyponatremia</b>	<b>Abnormally low sodium</b> in the blood (<i>hypo-</i> low + <i>natr-</i> sodium + <i>-emia</i> blood). Can lead to <b>seizures, coma, and death</b>.
Mechanism of <b>hyponatremia</b>	<b>Drinking too much water</b> while failing to intake proper <b>salts and electrolytes</b> — the body's salt content dilutes.
S/S of <b>Hyponatremia</b>	• <b>Altered mental status</b> — anxiety, irritability<br>• <b>Headache, weakness, fatigue, lightheadedness</b><br>• <b>Muscle cramps</b><br>• <b>Nausea, vomiting</b><br>• <b>Sweaty</b> skin<br>• <b>↑ urination</b>; <b>clear urine</b>
Tx of <b>Hyponatremia</b>	• <b>Stop activity</b> &amp; move patient to a <b>shady area</b> to rest<br>• <b>Restrict continued intake of water</b><br>• Encourage eating <b>salty snacks</b><br>• Option: <b>9% salt solution</b> — 3–4 <b>bouillon cubes</b> in 1/2 cup of water
Differential: <b>Hyponatremia vs. Heat Exhaustion</b> (by urine)	• <b>Hyponatremia</b> — <b>↑ urination, clear</b> urine (overhydrated, salt-diluted)<br>• <b>Heat Exhaustion</b> — <b>↓ urine output, dark yellow</b> urine (dehydrated)
What does <b>A&amp;Ox4</b> stand for, and what are the <b>4</b>?	<b>Alert and Oriented × 4</b> — the four orientation questions used to verify a patient is <b>fully alert</b> (the <b>A</b> of AVPU):<br><br><b>1. Person</b> — do they know who they are?<br><b>2. Place</b> — do they know where they are?<br><b>3. Time</b> — do they know the date / day?<br><b>4. Event</b> — do they know what happened?<br><br>Missing any of the four = <b>not A&amp;Ox4</b> = <b>altered mental status</b>. After head trauma, that alone constitutes a <b>TBI</b>.
What are the <b>4 things to consider</b> when assessing a <b>burn</b>?	• <b>Source</b> of the burn<br>• <b>BSA</b> — body surface area burned<br>• <b>Depth</b> of the burn<br>• <b>Patient</b> — risk factors
What are the <b>3 sources of burns</b>?	• <b>Thermal</b> — flame, radiation, heat exposure, steam, UV light, nuclear<br>• <b>Chemical</b> — acids, bases<br>• <b>Electrical &amp; lightning</b> — can cause unseen <b>internal injuries</b>
What is the <b>Rule of Palms</b>, and what is it used for?	A field method to estimate the <b>body surface area (BSA)</b> burned:<br><br>The <b>patient's palm ≈ 1% of their BSA</b>.<br><br>Compare the burn area to their palm to estimate percent BSA.
What are the <b>three depths of burns</b> — structures affected and skin signs?	<b>1° Superficial</b><br>• Structures: <b>epidermis</b> only<br>• Signs: sunburn — <b>red, hot, tender</b> skin<br><br><b>2° Partial Thickness</b><br>• Structures: <b>epidermis + dermis</b><br>• Signs: <b>blisters</b> present<br><br><b>3° Full Thickness</b><br>• Structures: <b>epidermis, dermis, &amp; subcutaneous</b><br>• Signs: <b>charred</b> skin — <b>black/dry or white</b>; possible <b>bone, muscle, organ, nerve</b> damage
Who is at <b>highest risk</b> from burns?	• <b>Very old &amp; very young</b> — heal slowly; more susceptible to <b>volume shock from dehydration</b><br>• Patients with a <b>history of medical problems</b><br>• Patients with <b>musculoskeletal problems</b>
Tx of <b>thermal burn</b>	• <b>Stop the burning process</b> immediately<br>• <b>Expose burnt skin</b> — <b>cut around stuck clothing</b> (don't peel it off); remove jewelry<br>• <b>Cool the burn</b> with cool flowing water or water-soaked gauze<br>• Consider <b>patient, location, depth, severity</b><br>• <b>Separate burned fingers/toes</b> with clean or sterile dressings<br>• Apply <b>dry sterile dressings</b> — keep area clean and protected
What <b>secondary problems should you anticipate</b> after a <b>thermal burn (A1–A4)</b>?	• <b>A1 — Airway</b>: smoke or gases inhaled?<br>• <b>A2 — Hypothermia</b>: thermoregulation is compromised<br>• <b>A3 — Infection</b>: large BSA or genital burn<br>• <b>A4 — Volume Shock</b>: burns cause severe dehydration
Tx of <b>electrical burn or lightning strike</b> (what to anticipate)	<b>First — ensure scene safety</b>: all current off, lightning subsided.<br><br>Then anticipate:<br>• <b>Internal injuries</b> — current travels through the body<br>• <b>Respiratory issues</b> from internal swelling<br>• <b>Cardiac arrest</b> from arrhythmia<br>• <b>Altered mental status</b> from brain injury<br>• <b>Contact / grounding burns</b> — often on <b>hands &amp; feet</b>
Tx of <b>chemical burn</b>	<b>Priority: remove the chemical quickly to minimize damage.</b><br><br>• <b>Liquid chemical</b> — immediately <b>flush</b> the area away from other skin and bystanders; continue for <b>at least 15 minutes</b><br>• <b>Dry chemical</b> — <b>brush it off</b>; do <b>NOT</b> add water to unknown dry chemicals (may react)<br>• Then apply <b>dry sterile dressings</b> and treat as a thermal burn
Evac for <b>burns</b>	• <b>High-risk burn</b> (large BSA, full thickness, face / genitals / joints)<br>• <b>Infection</b> present<br>• <b>Hypothermia</b><br>• Other <b>significant risks</b> present
What are the <b>three degrees of cold injury</b>, in order of progression?	<b>1. Frost Nip</b> — only outer epidermis affected; reversible<br><b>2. Superficial Frostbite</b> — water in tissue cells freezes; vessels damaged<br><b>3. Deep Frostbite</b> — skin and underlying tissues frozen solid
S/S of <b>Frost Nip</b>	• Only <b>outer layers of the epidermis</b> affected<br>• <b>Sensation intact</b> — pins/needles, tingling, throbbing<br>• <b>Skin color</b>: pale, white, sometimes red<br>• <b>Skin texture</b>: <b>soft and cool</b> to touch
S/S of <b>Superficial Frostbite</b>	• <b>Sensation dulled</b> — discomfort progressing to <b>loss of sensation</b><br>• <b>Skin color</b>: <b>blotchy</b>; turns from red → white/pale → bluish<br>• <b>Skin texture</b>: <b>firm, hard, and cold</b> to touch
Tx of <b>Frost Nip &amp; Superficial Frostbite</b>	• <b>Protect from exposure</b> &amp; reverse shell/core effect<br>• <b>Remove jewelry</b><br>• <b>Rewarm with skin-to-skin contact</b> — <b>avoid direct heat sources</b> (risk of burns)<br>• <b>Leave any blisters intact</b><br>• <b>Prevent re-cooling</b> of the extremity; keep patient warm
S/S of <b>Deep Frostbite</b>	• Skin &amp; underlying tissues are <b>frozen solid</b><br>• <b>Skin color</b>: <b>white-blue</b>; <b>firm</b>; <b>ice crystals visible</b><br>• <b>Sensation</b>: <b>numb</b>
Tx of <b>Deep Frostbite</b> (default)	<b>If risk of refreezing is high — do NOT rewarm.</b> Refreezing can be devastating.<br><br>• <b>Wrap and insulate</b> to prevent accidental thawing<br>• <b>Protect</b> the area from further injury<br>• <b>Controlled rewarming in a hospital</b> is the best method — <b>evacuate</b>
Tx of <b>Deep Frostbite</b> (field rewarming protocol)	Use only when there's <b>no way to evacuate without using the extremity</b>:<br><br>• <b>Pre-medicate</b> with <b>Ibuprofen 600 mg</b><br>• <b>Immerse</b> the area in <b>warm water for 20–30 min</b><br>• Water should be <b>warm, not hot</b>: <b>98.6–104°F</b><br>• <b>A1</b>: anticipate <b>severe pain, swelling, blistering, color changes</b><br>• After rewarming, <b>protect</b> and <b>do not allow to refreeze</b><br>• <b>Splint</b> the extremity &amp; <b>evacuate</b>
Define <b>Raynaud's Phenomenon</b>	A <b>disorder of the blood vessels</b> causing <b>vasoconstriction of the fingers &amp; toes</b>.<br><br>Anyone can develop it.
Predisposing factors for <b>Raynaud's</b>	• <b>Repetitive use injury</b><br>• <b>Vibration injury</b><br>• <b>Previous frostbite</b><br>• <b>Systemic illness</b>
Beyond <b>wound management &amp; function</b>, what else should you consider with a <b>facial injury</b>?	• <b>Patient stress</b> — facial injuries are <b>distressing</b>; reassure the patient<br>• <b>Airway</b> issues — bleeding, swelling, or debris can compromise the airway
Tx of <b>eye injury</b>	• <b>Cover the injured eye</b> with a <b>loose gauze pad</b> — <b>no pressure</b> on the eye itself<br>• <b>Cover the uninjured eye too</b> — prevents <b>bilateral eye movement</b> that worsens the injury<br>• <b>Reassure and guide</b> the patient — eye injuries are very distressing<br>• <b>EVACUATE any patient with an eye injury</b>
Tx of <b>chemical eye exposure</b>	• <b>Flush continuously</b> with <b>saline or water</b> for at least <b>15 seconds</b> upon initial exposure (longer if needed)<br>• <b>Remove contact lenses</b> and continue to flush<br>• <b>Flush away from the nose and the other eye</b> to avoid contamination
Tx of <b>eye infection</b>	• <b>Be mindful of contamination</b> — to other party members <b>and</b> to the patient's other eye<br>• <b>Flush and rinse</b> the eye regularly
Tx of <b>object lodged in the ear</b>	• <b>Leave the object in place</b> if it cannot be easily seen or removed<br>• If you suspect an <b>insect</b>:<br>&nbsp;&nbsp;– Place <b>2–3 small drops of room-temperature cooking oil</b> into the ear<br>&nbsp;&nbsp;– Have the patient lay on their side with the <b>affected ear toward the ground</b><br>• If only <b>small debris</b> is in the ear: carefully <b>irrigate with water</b>
Differential: <b>Middle vs. Outer Ear infection</b>	<b>Middle Ear</b><br>• <b>S/S</b>: <b>vertigo</b>; <b>no pain</b> when tugging on the ear lobe<br>• <b>Tx</b>: <b>evacuate</b><br><br><b>Outer Ear</b><br>• <b>S/S</b>: <b>pain when tugging</b> on the ear lobe; obvious <b>inflammation</b><br>• <b>Tx</b>: rinse daily with a solution of <b>50% water + 50% vinegar or alcohol</b>
Define <b>epistaxis</b>	A <b>nosebleed</b>.
Tx of <b>epistaxis</b>	• Have patient <b>sit up straight</b>, head <b>tilted forward</b>, chin down — <b>do not</b> tilt back or lay down (causes blood swallowing)<br>• <b>Pinch the soft portion</b> of the nose; hold <b>direct pressure</b><br>• If bleeding continues: gently <b>pack nostrils with gauze</b> soaked in <b>antibiotic ointment or saline</b><br>• Advise patient <b>not to blow their nose</b> — can dislodge forming scabs
Tx of <b>tooth avulsion</b>	A knocked-out <b>permanent adult tooth</b> can sometimes be saved by <b>re-implanting it</b>. <b>Time is critical.</b><br><br>• <b>Control bleeding</b> first — have patient <b>bite down on gauze</b><br>• <b>Hold the tooth by the crown</b>, <b>not the root</b>; leave in place if only partially knocked out<br>• <b>Rinse the tooth</b> with saline or the patient's saliva — <b>don't remove attached tissue</b><br>• <b>Rinse the socket</b> with saline<br>• <b>Press the tooth back</b> into the socket<br>• <b>Evacuate</b>
How do you <b>store an avulsed tooth</b> if you can't re-implant it?	• Keep the tooth <b>moist</b> by storing it in the <b>patient's saliva</b> or <b>milk</b><br>• <b>Never let the tooth become dry</b>
Assessment of <b>abdominal pain</b> (physical + associated S/Sx)	<b>Pain history:</b> use <b>OPQRST</b> for the pain itself.<br><br><b>Physical exam — palpate the 4 quadrants:</b><br>• <b>Tenderness</b> — localized vs. diffuse<br>• <b>Guarding</b> — voluntary protective flexion/hunching<br>• <b>Rigidity</b> — involuntary, board-like → suspect <b>peritonitis</b><br>• <b>Distension</b><br><br><b>Associated S/Sx:</b><br>• <b>N/V</b> — note any <b>blood</b> (red or coffee-ground)<br>• <b>Bowel changes</b> — diarrhea, constipation, <b>black tarry stool</b>, red blood<br>• <b>Fever / chills</b><br>• <b>Urine changes</b> — blood, pain, frequency<br>• <b>Pregnancy status</b> / last menstrual period<br>• <b>Signs of shock</b> — pale, cool, clammy, ↑ HR
Tx of <b>abdominal pain</b> (general field, undifferentiated)	You don't need a diagnosis to act — Tx is <b>protective</b>:<br><br>• <b>Position of comfort</b> — let the patient lie how they want (often knees drawn up to chest)<br>• <b>NPO or sips only</b> — generally <b>no food</b>; small sips of clear fluids if tolerated. Eating worsens a surgical abdomen<br>• <b>Monitor vital signs</b> for shock trending<br>• <b>Anticipate &amp; treat for shock</b> if signs develop<br>• <b>No laxatives, no anti-diarrheals, no aggressive pain meds</b> in the field — they can mask the picture or worsen obstruction/bleeds. NSAIDs are risky if GI bleed or ulcer is suspected<br>• <b>Reassess frequently</b> — the trend matters more than any single snapshot<br>• <b>Evacuate</b> based on red flags
Evac for <b>abdominal pain</b> (red flags)	<b>Evacuate if YES to any:</b><br><br>• Pain has persisted <b>&gt; 24 hours</b> or is <b>worsening</b><br>• <b>Fever</b><br>• <b>Persistent vomiting</b> / unable to keep fluids down<br>• <b>Bloody output</b> — vomit (red or coffee-ground), stool (red or black tarry), urine<br>• <b>Guarding</b> or <b>rigid abdomen</b><br>• <b>Signs of shock</b><br>• <b>Pregnancy suspected</b> (especially with bleeding) — ectopic risk<br>• <b>High-impact MOI</b> to the torso
S/S of <b>appendicitis</b>	• <b>Classic pain pattern</b>: dull, vague <b>periumbilical</b> pain that <b>migrates to the RLQ</b> over hours<br>• <b>Rebound tenderness</b> in the RLQ — pain worse on release than on press<br>• <b>Loss of appetite</b><br>• <b>N/V</b> (after pain onset, not before)<br>• <b>Low-grade fever</b><br>• <b>Guarding</b><br><br><b>Risk:</b> if untreated, the appendix can <b>rupture</b> → <b>peritonitis</b>.
Tx of <b>appendicitis</b>	<b>You cannot treat appendicitis in the field — it is a surgical emergency.</b><br><br>• <b>NPO</b> (nothing by mouth)<br>• <b>Position of comfort</b><br>• <b>Monitor</b> for shock and progression to peritonitis<br>• <b>Evacuate immediately</b>
Define <b>peritonitis</b>	Inflammation of the abdominal lining caused by a <b>perforation or rupture</b> (e.g. burst appendix, perforated ulcer, ruptured ectopic).
S/S of <b>peritonitis</b>	• <b>Severe, generalized abdominal pain</b><br>• <b>Rigid, board-like abdomen</b> (involuntary guarding)<br>• <b>Fever</b><br>• <b>N/V</b><br>• <b>Pain worsens with any movement</b> — patient lies very still<br>• Signs of <b>vascular shock</b> (sepsis) develop as it progresses
Tx of <b>peritonitis</b>	<b>Surgical emergency — life-threatening.</b><br><br>• <b>NPO</b><br>• <b>Position of comfort</b> — patient often lies still with knees drawn up<br>• <b>Anticipate &amp; treat for vascular shock</b> (sepsis)<br>• <b>Evacuate immediately</b> — go fast
When should you be <b>extra mindful</b> of <b>genito-urinary problems</b>?	• On <b>extended adventures</b><br>• When traveling where <b>water / washing is less available</b><br>• When traveling where <b>places to urinate / defecate are less available</b>
Define <b>hemorrhoids</b>	Swollen, inflamed veins of the <b>anus or lower rectum</b>. Common in both men and women.
S/S of <b>hemorrhoids</b>	• <b>Bright red blood</b> on stool or TP<br>• <b>Anal itching &amp; pain</b>
Tx of <b>hemorrhoids</b>	• <b>Hemorrhoid cream</b><br>• <b>Hydration</b>
What does <b>UTI</b> stand for?	<b>Urinary Tract Infection</b> — bacteria entering the urinary tract, causing infection. <b>More prevalent in women</b>, often caused by <b>urine retention</b>.
S/S of <b>UTI</b>	• <b>Strong, sudden urge</b> to urinate<br>• <b>Painful</b> or <b>cloudy</b> urination<br>• <b>Loss of bladder control</b>
Tx of <b>UTI</b>	<b>Prevention is key:</b><br>• <b>Drink lots of water</b><br>• Wear <b>loose-fitting cotton</b> underwear<br>• <b>Urinate when needed</b>; clean thoroughly after
Evac for <b>UTI</b>	Evacuate if <b>fever develops</b> or conditions <b>persist and worsen</b>.
Define <b>vaginitis</b>	Infection of the <b>exterior or interior</b> of the vagina.
S/S of <b>vaginitis</b>	• <b>Abnormal discharge</b> — white / yellow / brown<br>• <b>Itchy / burning</b> sensation
Tx of <b>vaginitis</b>	• Treat as you would a normal infection<br>• Clean regularly with a <b>diluted vinegar/water solution</b><br>• Use a <b>plastic syringe or sport-cap bottle</b> to clean inside the canal if needed<br>• <b>Hydrate</b>
Define <b>kidney stones</b>	A solid mass that forms in the kidney from a <b>buildup of minerals in urine</b>.
S/S of <b>kidney stones</b>	• <b>Blood</b> in urine<br>• <b>Smelly / cloudy</b> urine<br>• <b>Burning</b> urination<br>• <b>Fever / chills</b><br>• <b>Vomiting</b><br>• <b>Persistent pain</b> in back, side, lower belly, or groin
Tx of <b>kidney stones</b>	• <b>Drink lots of water</b><br>• Encourage <b>relaxation</b> and a <b>position of comfort</b>
Evac for <b>kidney stones</b>	Evacuate if pain has not resolved within <b>24 hours</b>.
Define <b>testicular torsion</b>	An abnormal fixation of the testes — often <b>twisted within the scrotum</b>. Common in puberty; may be triggered by running, odd sleeping position, or trauma.
S/S of <b>testicular torsion</b>	• <b>Acute unilateral pain</b><br>• <b>Swelling</b> or <b>high position</b> of the testicle<br>• <b>Nausea / vomiting</b>
Tx of <b>testicular torsion</b>	<b>Manual detorsion</b> — a <b>same-sex guide</b> should coach the patient to perform it.
Evac for <b>testicular torsion</b>	Evacuate if it lasts longer than <b>12 hours</b>.
Define <b>ectopic pregnancy</b>	A pregnancy implanted in the <b>fallopian tube</b>.
S/S of <b>ectopic pregnancy</b>	• <b>Pain in pelvis, abdomen, or sides</b><br>• <b>Nausea / vomiting</b><br>• <b>Abnormal vaginal bleeding or cramping</b><br>• <b>Weakness / fainting</b>
Tx of <b>ectopic pregnancy</b>	<b>Evacuate</b> — patient needs a doctor for diagnosis and treatment.
Evac for <b>genito-urinary problems</b> (general)	If <b>YES to any</b> of the following → <b>evacuate</b>:<br><br>• Has the problem persisted <b>more than 24 hours</b>?<br>• Does the patient have a <b>fever</b>, and are S/S or vital signs <b>worsening over time</b>?<br>• Can the patient still <b>urinate without pain</b>, or at all?<br>• Is <b>pregnancy suspected</b>?
Why should you have a <b>witness present</b> when assessing a <b>genito-urinary problem</b>?	To <b>protect both the rescuer and the patient</b> from <b>discomfort</b> or <b>legal problems</b>.
S/S of <b>simple rib fracture</b>	• <b>Point tenderness</b> at the fracture site<br>• <b>Pain on breathing, coughing, or movement</b><br>• <b>Shallow, guarded breathing</b> — patient avoids deep breaths<br>• Patient <b>holds or splints the area</b><br>• Possible <b>crepitus</b> (grating) at the site<br>• <b>A1:</b> anticipate progression to <b>pneumothorax</b> if the broken rib punctures the lung
Tx of <b>simple rib fracture</b>	• <b>Pain management</b><br>• <b>Encourage deep breathing</b> — counterintuitive, but prevents <b>atelectasis and pneumonia</b> from shallow breathing<br>• <b>Sling and swathe</b> the arm on the injured side for comfort and to limit movement<br>• <b>Never tape circumferentially</b> around the chest — restricts breathing<br>• <b>Monitor</b> for developing <b>pneumothorax</b> or <b>flail chest</b>
Define <b>flail chest</b>	<b>2 or more rib fractures</b> creating <b>instability</b> when breathing.
S/S of <b>flail chest</b>	<b>Uneven chest movement</b> while the patient is breathing.<br><br><b>A1:</b> a <b>floating piece of rib</b> may move on inhalation if the chest is left unstable.
Tx of <b>flail chest</b>	• <b>Tape padding</b> to the <b>injured side</b> and/or <b>sling/swathe the arm to the body</b><br>• <b>Never tape completely around the chest</b> — restricts breathing<br>• <b>P.R.O.P.</b><br>• <b>Evacuate immediately</b>
With a <b>chest wound</b> (puncture / impalement), what must you check during your assessment?	<b>Expose the wound site</b> and inspect the <b>other side of the chest and the back</b> for <b>exit wounds</b>.<br><br>A penetrating chest injury can also cause <b>internal injuries to the lungs</b>.
What <b>three escalating problems</b> should you anticipate with a chest wound (A1–A3)?	<b>A1 — Open puncture</b>: wound that allows <b>air to escape</b> the chest.<br><br><b>A2 — Sucking chest wound</b>: wound <b>traps air inside</b> the chest cavity → can cause <b>lung collapse</b>.<br><br><b>A3 — Tension pneumothorax</b>: caused by <b>completely sealing</b> the wound — pressure builds up as air is trapped between the chest wall and the lungs.
Tx of <b>open chest wound</b>	• Apply an <b>occlusive dressing</b> — lets <b>no air or water</b> pass<br>&nbsp;&nbsp;– If no commercial dressing, use a <b>glove</b> or <b>sterile plastic bag</b><br>&nbsp;&nbsp;– <b>Seal on all 4 sides</b> with medical tape<br>• <b>P.R.O.P.</b><br>• <b>Evacuate</b>
Define <b>flutter valve</b> (and when do you create one?)	<b>What:</b> an occlusive dressing sealed on <b>3 sides only</b> — air can <b>escape</b> on the open side but cannot re-enter.<br><br><b>When:</b> if breathing becomes <b>more difficult</b> after sealing a chest wound, <b>remove the tape from one side</b> to create the flutter valve. Prevents progression to <b>tension pneumothorax</b>.
What is the rule of <b>abandonment</b> in wilderness care?	Once you have <b>begun to treat</b> someone, you must <b>continue care</b> until <b>someone with equal or greater training</b> takes over.<br><br>If you leave, it is considered <b>abandonment</b> — you can be held <b>liable</b>.
What <b>5 developing problems</b> should you continually anticipate during <b>ongoing care</b>?	• <b>Infection</b><br>• <b>Hypothermia</b><br>• <b>Shock</b><br>• <b>Anxiety</b><br>• <b>Further injury</b>
How does <b>injury create pathways for infection</b>?	<b>Injury creates pathways for infection.</b> Three main routes:<br><br>• <b>Dirty objects</b> pierce skin and underlying tissue<br>• <b>Hollow organs</b> can be pierced and <b>spill infectious contents</b> into the body<br>• <b>The care you provide</b> — <b>dirty hands or unclean materials</b> can introduce infection
S/S of <b>septic / progressing infection</b>	• <b>Increased pain, redness, and swelling</b> at the wound site<br>• <b>Increased heat</b> at the wound site<br>• <b>Increased pus</b><br>• <b>Red streaks</b> just under the skin near the wound<br>• <b>Increased body temperature</b> (fever)<br>• <b>Poor skin signs</b> — <b>pale, grey, moist</b>
Tx of <b>developing infection</b> (in the field)	• <b>Allow the wound to reopen</b> and <b>let it drain</b><br>• Encourage drainage with <b>soaks in water as hot as the patient can tolerate</b><br>• Cover the wound with a dressing soaked in <b>clean water or saline</b><br>• Cover the wet dressing with a <b>dry dressing</b> to encourage further draining<br>• <b>Repeat 2× a day</b> during an extended evacuation<br>• <b>Anticipate &amp; treat for vascular shock</b><br>• <b>Evacuate quickly</b> for any serious infection — <b>antibiotics may be required</b>
Responder-safety considerations when treating an <b>infection</b>	• <b>Dispose of PPE and infected bandages/gauze carefully</b><br>• <b>Staph infections (e.g. MRSA)</b> can be transferred through <b>skin and fluid contact</b><br>• MRSA can only be treated effectively with <b>antibiotics</b><br>• <b>Pathogens may stay present on materials hours to days</b> after exposure
What are the leader's <b>overarching prevention principles</b> on a wilderness trip?	<b>Prevention is easier than dealing with an injury and evacuation.</b><br><br>• Make an <b>alternate plan</b><br>• <b>Continually assess</b> the energy and abilities of yourself and your group<br>• <b>Re-evaluate the itinerary</b> when you sense fatigue, injury, or danger<br>• <b>Note the weather, insects/vectors, and other conditions</b> and plan accordingly
Prevention of <b>blisters &amp; chafing</b>	• <b>Constantly check in and fix hot spots</b> before they become a problem<br>• <b>Leave blisters intact</b> to minimize infection<br>• <b>Remove shoes/socks and dry feet</b> when breaking<br>• Wear <b>loose-fitting absorbent</b> clothing
Prevention of <b>facial infection &amp; injury</b>	• <b>Wash hands frequently</b> and be mindful of personal hygiene<br>• Ensure <b>proper eyewear</b> is available — especially in the snow
Prevention of <b>burns</b> in the backcountry	• <b>Wear and reapply sunscreen</b>; use other sun protection<br>• Keep <b>boiling water carefully contained</b> — create a designated cooking space<br>• Establish a <b>designated distance</b> from the fire<br>• Always keep a <b>water source nearby</b> when there's fire
Risk factors for <b>drowning</b>	• <b>Swimmer panic</b> — cold water, swift water, exhaustion<br>• <b>Non-swimmers</b> falling into water<br>• <b>Seizure, stroke, or cardiac arrest</b> while swimming<br>• <b>Trauma</b> from unseen dangers in the water
Define <b>laryngospasm</b> (and when does it occur in a drowning?)	<b>Involuntary constriction</b> of the upper-airway muscles — <i>think hiccups</i>.<br><br>Occurs when someone is <b>submerged</b>; the body attempts to <b>protect the airway</b>.
What is the <b>Reach, Throw, Row, Go</b> water-rescue priority?	From safest to most dangerous:<br><br>• <b>Reach</b> — with a paddle or other device while staying safely on land<br>• <b>Throw</b> — a flotation device or line within the Pt's grasp, then pull to shore<br>• <b>Row</b> — out to the Pt from a watercraft, then use reach/throw<br>• <b>Go</b> — only if trained; swim a flotation aid to the actively struggling Pt
What is the <b>submersion window</b> in which a drowning Pt may recover with quick BLS?	<b>Less than 30 minutes</b> of submersion — quick, effective <b>BLS</b> may achieve recovery.
What does <b>BLS</b> stand for?	<b>Basic Life Support</b>.
Tx of <b>drowning</b> (Pt still breathing)	• Place in the <b>recovery position</b><br>• <b>Monitor the airway</b>
Tx of <b>drowning</b> (Pt unconscious &amp; not breathing)	• Begin <b>CPR</b> with an emphasis on <b>rescue breathing first</b><br>• Use an <b>AED</b> — always <b>dry the chest</b> before applying pads<br>• <b>Monitor the airway</b> and <b>anticipate vomiting</b>
Ongoing considerations after <b>any drowning</b> — breathing or not	• <b>Evacuate quickly</b> and carefully<br>• The Pt <b>may vomit water</b> even after being resuscitated
Define <b>terrain traps</b> (avalanche)	Features that <b>worsen the consequences</b> of being caught in an avalanche:<br><br>• <b>Gullies</b><br>• <b>Cliffs</b><br>• <b>Flattened areas</b> (deposition zones)<br><br>If you can't escape the path, <b>avoid moving toward terrain traps</b>.
If caught in an <b>avalanche</b>, what should you do <b>while still moving</b>?	• <b>Move to the side of the path</b> — avoid <b>terrain traps</b><br>• Try to <b>stay on your board or skis</b><br>• Keep your <b>legs downhill</b><br>• <b>"Swim"</b> to stay close to the top if you can't glide on top
If caught in an <b>avalanche</b>, what should you do <b>once the snow slows</b>?	• <b>Reach one hand</b> out toward the surface<br>• Use your <b>other arm</b> to create an <b>air bubble</b> around your face<br>• <b>Stay calm and still</b> until your partners unbury you
What <b>four anticipated patient problems (A1–A4)</b> should you expect with an <b>avalanche burial</b>, and what is the Tx for each?	<b>A1 — Respiratory failure</b> from asphyxiation → <b>open the airway</b>; <b>P.R.O.P.</b> &amp; <b>BLS</b><br><br><b>A2 — ICP</b> from trauma or hypoxia → <b>P.R.O.P.</b><br><br><b>A3 — Musculoskeletal trauma</b> → <b>stabilize the spine</b> and all unstable injuries<br><br><b>A4 — Hypothermia</b> → <b>hypothermia wrap</b>; warm fluids &amp; easy-to-consume sugars
What <b>core assumption</b> should guide your care in a <b>remote location</b>?	<b>Always assume the Pt's condition could deteriorate.</b>
When is it <b>OK to call for a helicopter rescue</b>?	When the <b>Pt's life is in danger</b> — it may be the <b>most appropriate</b> rescue.<br><br>Remember: helicopter rescue is <b>risky to all involved</b>.
If a helicopter lands and decides the Pt is <b>not critical</b> and doesn't need to fly, <b>who pays</b>?	<b>No one</b> — the helicopter will <b>not charge</b> you or the Pt for initiating the call.<br><br>So don't hesitate to call if the Pt's life may be in danger.
How should you <b>signal rescue personnel</b> to indicate your location?	• Take steps to signal responders to your <b>exact location</b><br>• Give <b>coordinates and landmarks</b> when possible<br>• For <b>air searches</b>: spell messages in <b>rocks or branches on open ground</b><br>• Consider carrying <b>non-electronic signaling devices</b> — flares, mirrors
What are the <b>physical parameters for a helicopter LZ</b>?	• <b>Level surface, firm terrain</b><br>• <b>Open</b> — free of overhanging hazards, power lines, dead trees<br>• <b>100 ft × 100 ft</b>, and <b>twice the distance from the tallest obstacle</b><br>• <b>Secure &amp; remove all loose objects</b>
What does <b>LZ</b> stand for?	<b>Landing Zone</b> — for a helicopter rescue.
What <b>three things must you NEVER do</b> around a helicopter?	• <b>Never approach</b> a helicopter without being <b>guided by helicopter personnel</b><br>• <b>Never approach or stand near the tail rotor</b><br>• <b>Never approach from uphill</b> of the helicopter
What does the <b>HOTSAW</b> mnemonic stand for, and when do you use it?	Use HOTSAW when <b>reporting an LZ location</b> to rescue personnel:<br><br><b>H</b> — Hazards<br><b>O</b> — Obstructions<br><b>T</b> — Terrain features<br><b>S</b> — Surface conditions<br><b>A</b> — Animals present<br><b>W</b> — Wind / Weather
What does <b>MCI</b> stand for?	<b>Mass Casualty Incident</b>.
Define <b>MCI</b>	<b>Any time Pts outnumber the responders.</b><br><br>Range: a school shooting with many Pts, or a <b>rafting accident with 3 Pts and only 1 responder</b>.
What does the <b>START</b> mnemonic stand for, and when do you use it?	<b>START</b> — the method used to triage Pts in an <b>MCI</b>:<br><br><b>S</b> — Simple<br><b>T</b> — Triage<br><b>A</b> — And<br><b>R</b> — Rapid<br><b>T</b> — Transit
In MCI triage, what <b>3 categories (RPM)</b> do you assess, and what <b>2 questions</b> in each?	<b>R — Respirations</b>: airway open? breathing present?<br><br><b>P — Perfusion</b>: radial pulse present? cap refill &lt; 2 sec?<br><br><b>M — Mental status</b>: able to lift one arm? able to open eyes?<br><br>Then assign a <b>triage color</b>.
What are the <b>4 MCI triage colors</b>, and what does each mean?	<b>IMMEDIATE</b> — life-threatening injury; needs immediate help or may die<br><br><b>DELAYED</b> — serious injury, <b>no life threat</b>; needs <b>splinting, wound management, or shock care</b><br><br><b>MINOR</b> — <b>walking wounded</b>; simple fractures, wounds, minor burns, or psychiatric / suicidal Pt<br><br><b>MORGUE</b> — <b>pulseless and non-breathing</b> (failed RPM)
Who is usually the <b>Incident Commander</b> in a large MCI?	The <b>Fire Chief</b>.
What role do <b>WFA providers</b> typically play in an MCI chain of command?	<b>Ground Crew</b> — providing medical care in any of the color triage groups, alongside <b>EMTs, nurses, and doctors</b>.<br><br>Ground crew acts <b>beneath the Unit Leader</b>.
What does <b>WFA</b> stand for?	<b>Wilderness First Aid</b>.
